Constant Product Market Maker (CPMM)

Notes on Uniswap v2 mechanics, fees, and a worked slippage example.

The Constant Product Market Maker (CPMM) is a very simple, yet effective, model for determining trade value of two assets within a liquidity pool. Although the name itself may create some fear in terms of perceived complexity, it is defined by the minimal equation x · y = k. Within this formula, x and y are the reserve quantities of two assets, which are paired together for any exchange to take place. The (instantaneous) price of one asset relative to the other is the ratio of reserves: pX in Y = y/x (and symmetrically pY in X = x/y).

What holds this equation together is the “k” on the other side, known as the invariant. The invariant is especially useful for ensuring that price is quoted along a curve because it is kept (approximately) constant during the swap calculation using the pre-trade state. As trades move the pool along that curve, the quoted price changes; arbitrageurs compare the pool price to external markets and help keep it aligned. The invariant during swaps ensures that when the quantity of one asset becomes a much larger weight compared to the other, the exchange rate for the more heavily provided asset decreases in comparison to the other.

Uniswap v2 & fees

In decentralized finance, a few protocols have used the CPMM formula, notably Uniswap v2. The more modern Uniswap design uses concentrated liquidity (still an AMM; not a central limit order book), which I’ll explore later.

Fee detail (v2): the swap fee is 0.30% on the input amount. Pricing uses an effective input dxeff = dx · (1 − 0.003), but after the swap the pool receives the full input dx. Because a bit of input is retained in the pool each swap, the product x·y creeps up slightly over time.

The fee compensates LPs for providing liquidity. More liquidity (larger reserves) reduces price impact, which is one component of slippage, while the 0.30% fee itself is constant as a percentage of input.

Worked example

Say John wants to swap asset X for asset Y in a CPMM. The pool has x = 100 and y = 100, so k = 10,000. John swaps dx = 10 X. The pool charges a 0.30% fee, so the effective amount used in pricing is dxeff = 9.97. 

Pricing reserves: x + dx_eff = 109.97
y' (pricing)    = k / (x + dx_eff) = 10,000 / 109.97 = 90.9339
dy to John      = y − y' = 9.0661 Y

John exchanged 10 X for 9.0661 Y. The pre-trade mid-price was y/x = 1 (Y per X). The execution price was dy/dx = 0.90661 (Y per X), so total slippage vs the pre-trade mid is about 9.34% (≈ 0.30% fee + ≈ 9.04% price impact).

After settlement in v2 the new reserves are x' = x + dx = 110 and y' = y − dy = 90.9339. The new mid-price becomes y'/x' ≈ 90.9339 / 110 ≈ 0.8267.

Arbitrage keeps pools aligned

If these two assets are only priced by their reserve ratios, why don’t prices drift wildly relative to other markets? Arbitrageurs compare the pool’s price to external venues and trade whenever there’s a gap, which pulls the AMM back in line. Whether markets are perfectly efficient is debated, but they are among the closest we have—and new technology continuously pushes them closer.
